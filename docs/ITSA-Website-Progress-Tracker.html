1. Read this first

One task is blocking the entire project

The security rules that protect our database were written, tested, and merged weeks ago — but they were never switched on for the live database, because the Firebase credentials were never added to .env.local. Right now the live site still accepts changes from any signed-in Google account, and because the repository is public, that weakness is readable by anyone.

It also blocks the team: nobody can build or test an admin feature against real rules until this is done. It is tasks P0-1 through P0-4, roughly 30 minutes of console work, and it is owned by the project manager.

The real reason this redesign exists

The Google Drive problem is not a Drive problem. It is that three of our four content types cannot be published without a developer.

ContentAdmin pageDatabase ruleWhat it takes to publish today
EventsYesYesAn officer fills a form. Working.
OfficersYesYesWorks — but 19 officers are still written into the code as a fallback.
NewsNoNoA developer edits code, opens a PR, and deploys — per article.
ProjectsNoNoSame — per project.

There is a second blocker: there is no way to upload an image anywhere on the site. Events and officers accept an image link, not a file, and for security only our own storage is allowed to serve pictures — but nothing exists to put a picture into that storage. So a Drive folder of event photos currently has no route onto the website at all.

Everything in Phase 1 exists to fix exactly that. The redesign has landed when an officer who cannot code can take a folder from the Drive and publish it.

6. How we work — Scrum

Scrum, adapted for a volunteer student team. Textbook Scrum assumes people are available all day; we are not. Everything below is scaled to roughly six to eight hours a week per person, around classes.

The one rule that keeps this honest: the people doing the work estimate the work. The project manager sets priority and decides what "done" means; the developers decide how big something is. A manager who estimates for the team ends up with numbers nobody believes and a plan nobody can hit.

6.1 Roles

Scrum roleWhoResponsible for
Product Owner AJ Salinas Owns the backlog and its order. Decides what is most valuable next, writes acceptance criteria, and accepts or rejects finished work. Single point of decision when priorities collide.
Scrum Master Alexander Tolosa Runs the four ceremonies, keeps them short, and — most importantly — clears blockers. When someone says "I'm stuck on X," this is the person who gets X unstuck.
Development team Alexander, Aziel, Deghne, Twice, Matthew Estimates the work, commits to a sprint, and decides how to build it. The team collectively owns the sprint — not each person owning only their own rows.

The Product Owner and Scrum Master should not be the same person. The PO pushes for more scope; the SM protects the team's capacity. Putting both in one head means one of the two jobs quietly stops happening — usually the second.

6.2 Sprint cadence

Two-week sprints. One week is too short to finish anything meaningful around a class schedule; a month is too long to notice you have drifted.

CeremonyWhenHow longPurpose
Sprint Planning Monday, start of sprint 45 min PO presents the priority order; the team estimates and pulls in what it believes it can finish. Ends with a written sprint goal.
Written stand-up Mon / Wed / Fri, in Discord 2 min each Three lines: what I finished, what I am on, what is blocking me. Written, not a meeting — five students cannot reliably be in the same room daily, and pretending otherwise just produces skipped stand-ups.
Backlog refinement Mid-sprint, Wednesday 30 min Look at what is coming next sprint. Split anything too big, add acceptance criteria, surface unknowns while there is still time to investigate.
Sprint Review Friday, end of sprint 30 min Demo on the live preview link, not on a laptop. If it cannot be shown working, it is not done.
Retrospective Straight after the review 20 min What helped, what got in the way, one thing we change next sprint. Exactly one — teams that pick five change nothing.

Total ceremony overhead: about two hours per fortnight per person. If it grows past that, the Scrum Master should cut something rather than let the team quietly start skipping meetings.

6.4 Estimating

Story points, sized against each other rather than against hours. The rubric below is calibrated to student evenings, which is the unit that actually exists here.

PointsFeels likeExample from this project
1Under an hourAnswer the membership fee question (Q3)
2One eveningFix the news carousel pause (Z7)
3Two or three eveningsDatabase collections and rules for news and projects (T1)
5Most of a week around classesThe image upload service (T2)
8Too big — split it"Build the whole admin area" is not a task

Do not commit to a velocity you have not measured. Plan Sprint 1 conservatively — around 15 to 20 points across the whole team — then use what actually got finished as the number for Sprint 2. Two sprints in, the average is real and planning becomes accurate. Guessing high in Sprint 1 and missing it teaches the team that the plan is fiction.

6.5 Definition of Ready

A task may not enter a sprint until all of these are true:

  • It has a named owner.
  • It has acceptance criteria — a sentence describing what must be true for it to be accepted.
  • Its dependencies are already Done, not merely planned.
  • It is estimated at 5 points or fewer. Bigger items get split first.
  • Anything it needs — a design, a decision, an asset, an account — exists.

That last point is what most often bites this project: several tasks depend on the Firebase credentials in P0-1, and none of them are Ready until those exist.

6.6 Definition of Done

Unchanged from how the team already works — a task is Done when:

  • It works on a phone as well as a laptop.
  • It was built on a branch and merged through a pull request.
  • The automatic checks pass — lint, build, security rules, package audit.
  • Someone else reviewed it.
  • It has been clicked through on the live site after merging.
  • Its row in this tracker says Done, with the date.

Task P0-7 turns points two and three from something everyone must remember into something the repository enforces. Four people have pushed straight to the live branch so far; one of those pushes stopped the site from publishing for a day.

Three adaptations to hold on to

Stand-ups are written, three times a week. Five students with different timetables cannot reliably meet daily. A written stand-up that actually happens beats a daily meeting that does not.

Plan around exams explicitly. When a sprint overlaps assessments, halve the committed points at planning rather than discovering it at review. A sprint that is honestly small is a success; a sprint that is optimistically large is a failure with extra steps.

Sprint 5 is not optional. Backups, roles, and the handover note are what let next year's officers run this site. They are the easiest work to postpone and the most expensive to skip.

8. Definition of done for the whole redesign

  1. The database rules are live, and at least two officers can sign in.
  2. An officer who cannot code can take a folder from the Drive — photos and all — and publish it as an event, a news post, or a project.
  3. Nothing on the public site is placeholder text, an invented name, or a sample logo.
  4. The site loads acceptably on a mid-range phone on campus wifi.
  5. Someone other than the author has checked the site with a keyboard only, and for colour contrast.
  6. A handover note exists, and next year's officers can run the site without any of us.

Point 2 is the one that matters. Everything in Phase 1 and Phase 2 exists to make it true, and if it is still false at the end of term, the redesign did not land — however good the site looks.
